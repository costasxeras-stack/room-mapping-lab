How much room mapping to automate, and what each choice is worth.

Every supplier names the same room differently. Mapping them to one master room is what makes rates comparable, so travellers see one room with the best price instead of five confusing ones. This lab takes supplier room names and master rooms from the LiteAPI sandbox, tests three matching methods against LiteAPI's own mapping, and turns the confidence threshold into a business decision.

Three matching methods, scored against LiteAPI's mapping

Each method picks a master room for every supplier name and says how sure it is (0 to 1). The reference is the room LiteAPI mapped the rate to. It is LiteAPI's answer, not a hand-checked truth, so disagreement can mean either side is wrong.

  • Text similarity: character trigram overlap between the supplier name and each master room name.
  • Hybrid: high confidence when rules and similarity pick the same room, low when they disagree.

What moves the answer

How I would run mapping as a product

The outcome

Every bookable rate shows up as the right room, so travellers compare like with like and partners trust the content. Mapping is not the goal. Comparable, bookable rates are.

Metrics

  • North star: share of bookable rates mapped correctly, weighted by booking volume. A mapped rate nobody books matters less than one on page one.
  • Coverage: share of rate names mapped, by city and supplier.
  • Precision: share of mappings that are right, from a weekly audit sample, not from agreement.
  • Time to map: hours from a new hotel or supplier appearing to 95% coverage.
  • Guardrail: wrong-room complaints and refunds per 10,000 bookings.

The first 90 days

  1. Weeks 1 to 3: measure before building. A weekly audit sample of [n] mappings to get real precision by method and supplier. Size the cost of a wrong room with support and finance.
  2. Weeks 4 to 8: set thresholds by risk, not one number. Stricter where rooms are expensive or easy to confuse (suites, views), looser for simple doubles and twins. Route only the uncertain middle to people or to an LLM judge.
  3. Weeks 9 to 12: close the loop. Every human correction becomes training data and a rule candidate. Report coverage, precision and time to map per supplier, so the worst suppliers get fixed at the source.

Trade-offs I would put to engineering

  • Rules versus models: rules are cheap and explainable but plateau. Models need labelled data, which the review queue produces for free.
  • LLM judge: only on the uncertain slice, with a cost cap per 1,000 calls and a latency budget, so it never sits on the booking path.
  • Batch versus real time: map in batch when a supplier feed changes, and fall back to "unmapped but bookable" at search time instead of blocking.

The decision I would ask for

Agree the cost of a wrong room and the value of a mapped rate with finance, then set the auto-map threshold per room risk class from that, and review it monthly against the audit sample.

About this lab

  • Data comes from the LiteAPI sandbox through a GitHub job that uses a private key stored as a repository secret. The page never sees the key. Sandbox content and rates may differ from production.
  • The reference is LiteAPI's own mapping (mappedRoomId with room mapping on), not a hand-checked truth.
  • Business inputs are examples, not Nuitée figures. Nuitée is not involved in this project.
